## What did I learn?

I developed a more practical understanding of OOP by asking which object should own a rule, which details callers should know, and what should remain unchanged when a feature is added. These questions helped me evaluate the implementation beyond whether the game ran.

**Abstraction and polymorphism give callers a stable contract.** The abstract `Customer` class holds the shared lifecycle and exposes methods for the behaviour that varies: waiting loss, menu choice, billing and reaction to service. `Waiter.takeOrder` calls `customer.chooseMenuItem(menu)` and `customer.calculateBillCents(...)` through a `Customer` reference. Dynamic dispatch selects the appropriate behaviour. My design criterion was that adding a customer type should not require another type-checking branch in the controller. The base class preserves the shared lifecycle, while subclasses override the relevant behaviour. This gives inheritance a purpose beyond reusing fields.

**Encapsulation protects rules as well as fields.** Private state is useful only if the available methods preserve its invariants. `Order` controls its status transitions, while the Waiter checks readiness before serving. `Customer` owns its lifecycle guards and keeps satisfaction within 0 to 100. `Restaurant.seatFromQueue` removes a customer only after seating succeeds. I used these examples to judge whether an invalid action could leave the game partly changed. Checks beside the state they protect apply to every caller, including callers outside the CLI.

**Cohesion and coupling affect how easily a design changes.** A controller that parses input, selects customers, executes tasks and formats messages has several reasons to change. Separating those responsibilities made each class easier to explain and reduced the knowledge shared between the screen and controller. I also learned that a UML diagram should communicate these responsibilities accurately; listing classes alone does not establish a sound design.

## How did I apply it?

- **Combining inheritance with composition.** I retained `ComboMeal` as a subtype of `MenuItem` that holds references to two existing menu items. Inheritance expresses that a combo can be used wherever a menu item is expected; the component references express what it contains. Its price is 90% of the combined prices and its preparation units are their sum. `Chef`, `Cashier` and `Menu` needed no Stage 3 changes. I understood this as practical evidence of substitutability and the Open/Closed Principle: existing consumers handle the extension through their existing contract. Small setup changes, such as registering the combos in `Restaurant`, were still necessary.
- **Giving the agreed bill one owner.** I accepted price locking at order time: the Waiter asks the customer for its price, applies Happy Hour, and stores the result in `Order`. A VIP's Burger during Happy Hour is $15 x 0.9 x 0.8 = $10.80. The Cashier collects the stored amount. My reason for retaining this approach was temporal consistency: a promotion ending before payment should not change an already agreed bill. It also keeps the Cashier independent of customer-specific pricing and promotion timing.
- **Choosing the right amount of abstraction.** I retained a concrete `HappyHour` class rather than adding a `PricingPolicy` interface. The game has one promotion policy, so I judged that another interface would add indirection without solving a current variation problem. I recognised the trade-off: the Waiter depends on that concrete class. If another interchangeable policy were required, I would reconsider the boundary and extract an abstraction. This taught me to justify interfaces through actual responsibilities and variation.
- **Separating responsibilities and protecting ownership.** I chose to address the controller's accumulated responsibilities in the Stage 3 clean-up. Parsing moved to `ManagerCommand`, task execution to `StaffActions`, and `Restaurant` became the source of eligible-customer lists for both control and display. The waiting queue became read-only to callers, and the unused static order counter was removed. This reduced `GameController` from 324 to 150 lines and left it focused on turn sequencing. My acceptance criterion was improved structure without changed gameplay; the recorded verification includes byte-identical victory, defeat and invalid-input transcripts before and after refactoring.
- **Making extension boundaries verifiable.** I required each stage tag to run independently and used the README comparisons to explain why files changed or stayed unchanged. The checks also reject customer-type references in `GameController`, `StaffActions` and `ManagerCommand`. Together with tests of customer behaviour, these provide evidence for the intended separation of responsibilities. I learned to support an OOP claim with both code structure and observable behaviour.

## What challenged me?

The hardest part was reconciling the intended design with the implementation while keeping the code, diagrams, README and three tags consistent. A review finding did not automatically determine which artefact should change; I had to consider the responsibility involved and the consequences of moving it.

- **Diagrams ahead of the code.** The early Stage 2 and 3 diagrams placed `Customer.canFinishEating`, `Customer.canPay` and `Restaurant.findOldestEligiblePayer` in the controller. I had to decide whether the code should follow the drawing or whether the existing placement better protected domain responsibilities.
- **Drawing within the tool's limits.** Without Graphviz, PlantUML used its built-in Smetana layout. Multiplicity labels collided near `Customer`, and `ComboMeal` inheritance and aggregation lines overlapped. I needed to improve readability while preserving the intended relationships.
- **Representing staged development clearly.** The implementation commits were concentrated into a short period, and later-stage diagram files were already present in `stage-1` because the diagrams were prepared first. Independently runnable tags demonstrate the source boundaries, but the history communicates the development process less clearly than it could.
- **A bug the recorded runs missed.** `SERVE` or `COOK` without a customer number selected from all customers who had ordered, rather than those eligible for that task. The recorded runs always supplied the number, so they did not exercise that path.

## How did I solve it?

- **Resolve disagreements through responsibility.** I kept eating and payment eligibility on `Customer`, because those rules depend on its status and turn information. I kept payer selection on `Restaurant`, because it owns the customer collection. Moving them into the controller merely to match a draft would increase its knowledge of domain details. I therefore corrected the diagrams. Member-by-member comparisons against the compiled classes then exposed missing attributes and operations, which were reconciled for each stage. This separated the design decision about where a rule belongs from the mechanical check that the diagram describes it correctly.
- **Make diagram compromises explicit.** After trying alternative ranks and link placements, I put multiplicities in link labels and coloured the `ComboMeal` aggregation orange, documenting the layout compromise. I subsequently corrected the `Table` to `Customer` multiplicity to `0..1` and the queue notation across the diagrams. I treated readability and semantic accuracy as separate concerns that both needed attention.
- **Verify the stage boundaries.** I preserved commit timestamps and focused on making the stages independently usable: Stage 1 Java source excludes the later customer types and Stage 3 features, while Stage 2 excludes Stage 3 classes. Clean `git archive` builds and each tag's own checks provided recorded verification. The README explains the additions and modifications, including the Stage 3 refactoring.
- **Reuse the domain's eligibility rules.** I addressed the shortcut bug by having `StaffActions` use `Restaurant.getCustomersToServe()` and `getCustomersToCook()`. This applies the same rules to explicit and implicit customer selection. Five additional checks cover the correction, including the ambiguous case where two orders are READY (`983cc8a`). The fix reinforced why eligibility should have one owner instead of being reconstructed separately by each caller.

**What I would do differently.** I would record the rationale for each core decision when making it, including alternatives considered and why I rejected them. I would develop and reconcile each stage's diagram alongside its implementation, with commits that make the progression easier to follow. I would also test commands both with and without arguments and start the written design discussion and video script earlier. This project strengthened my ability to explain and defend an OOP design: identify the owner of a rule, define a useful abstraction, evaluate the cost of a dependency, and verify that an extension preserves existing behaviour.
