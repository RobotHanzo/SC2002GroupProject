Java command-line turn-based restaurant management game  |  Student brief
Your group builds a playable restaurant management game in Java. The player is the manager. Staff time is limited, so the manager decides which customers and orders come first. You build the game in three stages, and each stage must show how your object-oriented design supports the next set of requirements.

2.2  General rules
Customers choose their own food. The manager never chooses it. Use a documented deterministic rule or seeded randomness, and show the choice once the order is placed.
Keep rules out of the UI. The UI only collects the manager’s decisions and displays the state. Game rules belong in domain and control objects.
Money. If fractional prices arise, store amounts as integer cents. Display dollars with two decimals.
Offline. All mandatory features must work without network access.
3  How a turn works
3.1  Start-of-turn display
At the start of every turn, show: the turn number, revenue, average satisfaction of paid customers, the waiting queue, occupied tables, each customer’s satisfaction, order status, and the staff tasks available.
3.2  Turn steps
Each turn then runs these six steps in order.
Step
What happens
1  Arrivals
The scheduled customer joins the waiting queue with satisfaction 100.
2  Manager choices
Waiter: choose at most one task, or Wait: seat one queued customer at a free table, take one seated customer’s order, or serve one READY order.
Chef: choose at most one order to advance by one preparation unit, or Wait. The Chef may work on an order the Waiter took this turn.
3  Kitchen and serving
The Waiter acts before the Chef. When an order’s last preparation unit is done, it becomes READY. A dish can be served only if it was already READY when the Waiter acts, so a dish finished this turn is served next turn at the earliest. Serving sets it to SERVED.
4  Eating and payment
A customer served on an earlier turn finishes one full turn of eating and becomes READY_TO_PAY. The Cashier automatically handles at most one READY_TO_PAY customer per turn, oldest first. A customer who became READY_TO_PAY this turn pays next turn at the earliest. Payment adds the bill to revenue, marks the customer and order PAID, and frees the table.
5  Waiting and abandonment
At turn end, every waiting or seated customer who has not been served loses satisfaction (Regular: 8 points, minimum 0). A customer who reaches 0 leaves without paying: cancel any unfinished order and free the table. Customers who are served, eating or ready to pay lose nothing.
6  Result
The game always runs all 18 turns; there is no early ending. After turn 18: Victory if all three targets in Section 2.1 are met, otherwise Defeat.

A Waiter completes at most one task per turn.
A table stays occupied until its customer pays or leaves.
A customer cannot eat before being served. The Cashier cannot charge before READY_TO_PAY.

4.3  Stage 3: New role and menu policy
Host. The Host seats at most one queued customer per turn, in addition to the Waiter’s task. The Waiter can still seat customers.
ComboMeal. A combo is made of two existing menu items.
Preparation units = the sum of its two components.
Price = 90% of the components’ combined price, rounded to the nearest cent. Example: Salad + Pasta = ($9 + $18) × 0.9 = $24.30, 3 units.
A customer chooses a combo according to a rule you document.
Happy Hour. A pricing policy the manager may switch on.
Can be activated once per game, at the start of a turn. It lasts for that turn and the next. Display its remaining duration.
Orders taken while it is active get 20% off their bill.
During each active turn, customers still waiting for a table or food recover 15 satisfaction at turn start (maximum 100).
Apply the VIP discount first, then Happy Hour. The price is locked when the order is taken. Example: a VIP orders a Burger during Happy Hour: $15 × 0.9 × 0.8 = $10.80.
It does not change orders already placed, and does not affect customers who have left.
Stage 3 is independently playable.

What did I learn?

I gained a better understanding of how OOP principles can be applied to accommodate
real life changing software requirements. I learned inheritance, polymorphism and
abstraction support the Open/Closed Principle by allowing new functionality to be
introduced without extensively modifying existing classes. I also improved my ability to
construct UML class diagrams and represent relationships between classes including
inheritance and associations. The project helped me see how they contribute to designing
a real system outside of only theory in lectures.

How did I apply it?

My main contribution was designing parts of Stage 2 and 3 UML class diagrams and
explaining how I implemented the SOLID principles.

For Stage 2, I focused on introducing VIPCustomer and CriticCustomer as subclasses of
the abstract Customer class. This allowed different customer types to have specialized
behaviors such as waiting satisfaction penalties, discounts and ordering preferences while
following the same general restaurant workflow.

For Stage 3, I focused on extending the menu system through ComboMeal which inherits
from MenuItem and contains two existing menu items. This design allows combo meals to
be treated as menu items withing the existing ordering structure.

These designs help me apple lecture content and understand the importance of designing
classes that can accommodate new requirements.

What challenged me?

One of the biggest challenges was determining how to incorporate the new requirements
into the existing class structure without creating unnecessary dependencies or modifying
too many classes. I initially found it difficult to decide whether new features should be
modelled as subclasses or handled by modifying existing classes.

Another challenge was constructing and presenting the UML diagrams. As more classes,
methods and relationships were introduced, the diagrams became increasingly
complicated and difficult to read. I also had limited experience using Google Colab and
Mermaid for UML diagrams, which made generating, formatting and exporting the
diagrams challenging.
How did I solve it?

To address the design challenges, I revisited the concepts of inheritance, polymorphism
and SOLID principles, particularly OCP and LSP. I examined how customer-specific
behaviors could be encapsulated within subclasses rather than implemented using
repeated conditional checks in the controller.

Similarly, for the Stage 3 menu extension, I used the existing MenuItem abstraction to
represent ComboMeal, allowing the design to reuse the ordering structure.

For the diagram-related difficulties, I experimented with different rendering and export
methods in Google Colab. After encountering issues with diagram scaling and image
generation, I exported the diagrams as PNG files instead of relying on screenshots. I also
organised the diagrams and design documentation for inclusion in the group's GitHub
repository and final report.

This experience taught me that designing extensible software requires more than applying
inheritance mechanically. It involves understanding each class's responsibility,
considering the impact of new requirements on existing components, and making design
decisions that maintain clarity and flexibility.
